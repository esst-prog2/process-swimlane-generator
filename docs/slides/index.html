[0:00–0:45] Think about a request for supplies. Someone describes what they need, Purchasing checks it, Finance checks the budget, and Receiving accepts delivery. If those steps are already in Excel, drawing all the boxes and arrows again is extra work. My program turns a task list into a diagram grouped by department. It is for people who need to explain a straightforward process to colleagues. You supply the tasks, departments and next-task IDs. It creates a shareable SVG and an offline HTML viewer. The present version handles one linear chain, on Windows. It does not support yes/no branches yet. Let me show one handoff.

[2:15–3:20] A successful-looking diagram is not enough evidence. In Homework 5, I specified a separate four-task example before the run. The expected connections were A to B, B to C, and C to D. The test checks where the actual drawn arrows meet the task boxes. It does not simply trust the renderer's source and target labels. Then we deliberately changed the renderer to point two positions ahead. That produced A to C, B to D, and C to A. The independent test failed. After restoring the correct destination, it passed. These red and green outcomes were reproduced for this deck, with the faulty renderer isolated in a temporary copy. My control was deciding the expected result independently and checking whether the test could detect a real mistake, rather than accepting the agent's statement that everything worked.

Its placeholder names showed that the program could render a large example, but a newcomer had no familiar story to follow. The teacher pointed that out. I ran office-hours, reviewed the alternatives, and chose B: explain the purchase request before the viewer. You can see that decision in PROCESS row 9 and commit 9a99e13. The bigger stress test stayed lower on the page. My lesson is to choose the demo around what a first-time visitor needs to understand. A familiar request and one clear change of responsibility make the output easier to explain.
